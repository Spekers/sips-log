Meikyoku Kissa Lion, Tokyo. It was 2014. I had spent most of my life and career obsessed with music as a radio host, TV presenter, DJ and documentary filmmaker. Yet somehow, somewhere along the way, I had forgotten how to listen.It wasn't simply the music that moved me. It was the act of listening itself.I soon discovered that CafÃ© Lion wasn't an exception. Across Tokyo, and eventually in every corner of Japan, I found an extraordinary culture of ongaku kissa, or listening cafÃ©s. These weren't places where music drifted into the background while people caught up over coffee. They were spaces built around a simple but profound idea: that music is worthy of our complete attention. Listening wasn't treated as something passive. It was an experience, a ritual and, in many ways, an art form. That first afternoon at CafÃ© Lion became the beginning of a journey that would eventually become A Century in Sound, a six-part documentary series tracing one hundred years of music and modern Japanese history through the stories of the remarkable people who have dedicated their lives to the art of listening. 





The first photo I took of Jazz Kissa Eigakan in September 2015.A Decade in the MakingOn subsequent visits to Japan, I found myself falling deeper and deeper into this world of ongaku kissa. My days were spent buried in books, then climbing onto my bicycle and crossing Tokyo in search of the listening cafÃ©s I had uncovered through my research. The more time I spent inside them, the more I realised they were about far more than just music. They were places built by wonderful men and women who had quietly devoted their lives to sharing what they loved with others.What began as the idea for a single documentary gradually evolved into a six-part series. It would take nearly a decade to make. After years of development, we were finally able to begin filming. Then everything suddenly fell apart. Funding disappeared. Plans changed. There were moments when it seemed the project might never be realised. Yet every time I stepped back inside one of these cafÃ©s, I was reminded exactly why these people deserved to have their remarkable story told.





Jazz Kissa Eigakan owners Masahiro Yoshida and Kinuko Watanabe.Eventually, thanks to the generosity of a Japanese patron who shared our belief in the project, and together with an extraordinary team of collaborators, we were able to bring the series back to life. More and more people from around the world were discovering Japan's ongaku kissa for themselves. Many returned home inspired to create their own version of what they had experienced: places where the record was elevated, the sound was carefully considered and listening became the main event. Before long, listening bars inspired by Japan's ongaku kissa began appearing across Europe, North America, Australia and beyond.





A Century in Sound at the Barbican, London, 2026.Along the way, the first three episodes of A Century in Sound have screened at international festivals and sold-out events around the world, where the enthusiastic response has reinforced our belief that this culture of listening resonates far beyond Japan.
