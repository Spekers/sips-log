Join Us in Celebrating the Art of Listening

In an era where music accompanies almost every moment of our day, whether we are commuting, working, studying, exercising or scrolling, it's easy to forget that listening to music can be an experience in itself.

That hit me like a tonne of bricks the first time I walked into CafÃ© Lion, Japan's oldest classical music cafÃ©. Inside, the room was silent. Strangers sat facing a towering speaker system and giving the record playing their absolute, undivided attention. For more than a century, people had come here for one reason.

To listen.

Meikyoku Kissa Lion, Tokyo. 

It was 2014. I had spent most of my life and career obsessed with music as a radio host, TV presenter, DJ and documentary filmmaker. Yet somehow, somewhere along the way, I had forgotten how to listen.

It wasn't simply the music that moved me. It was the act of listening itself.

I soon discovered that CafÃ© Lion wasn't an exception. Across Tokyo, and eventually in every corner of Japan, I found an extraordinary culture of ongaku kissa, or listening cafÃ©s. These weren't places where music drifted into the background while people caught up over coffee. They were spaces built around a simple but profound idea: that music is worthy of our complete attention. Listening wasn't treated as something passive. It was an experience, a ritual and, in many ways, an art form. 

That first afternoon at CafÃ© Lion became the beginning of a journey that would eventually become A Century in Sound, a six-part documentary series tracing one hundred years of music and modern Japanese history through the stories of the remarkable people who have dedicated their lives to the art of listening.

A Decade in the Making

On subsequent visits to Japan, I found myself falling deeper and deeper into this world of ongaku kissa. My days were spent buried in books, then climbing onto my bicycle and crossing Tokyo in search of the listening cafÃ©s I had uncovered through my research. The more time I spent inside them, the more I realised they were about far more than just music. They were places built by wonderful men and women who had quietly devoted their lives to sharing what they loved with others.

What began as the idea for a single documentary gradually evolved into a six-part series. It would take nearly a decade to make. After years of development, we were finally able to begin filming. Then everything suddenly fell apart. Funding disappeared. Plans changed. There were moments when it seemed the project might never be realised. Yet every time I stepped back inside one of these cafÃ©s, I was reminded exactly why these people deserved to have their remarkable story told.

Jazz Kissa Eigakan owners Masahiro Yoshida and Kinuko Watanabe.

Eventually, thanks to the generosity of a Japanese patron who shared our belief in the project, and together with an extraordinary team of collaborators, we were able to bring the series back to life. By then, I had spent years as a regular at the cafÃ©s featured in the series, and at many more across the city. The friendships and trust that had grown over that time allowed us to document this remarkable culture with a level of intimacy that simply wouldn't have been possible otherwise.

During those same years, something else was happening. More and more people from around the world were discovering Japan's ongaku kissa for themselves. Many returned home inspired to create their own version of what they had experienced: places where the record was elevated, the sound was carefully considered and listening became the main event. Before long, listening bars inspired by Japan's ongaku kissa began appearing across Europe, North America, Australia and beyond.

A Century in Sound at the Barbican, London, 2026.

Along the way, the first three episodes of A Century in Sound have screened at international festivals and sold-out events around the world, where the enthusiastic response has reinforced our belief that this culture of listening resonates far beyond Japan. More than ten years after walking into CafÃ© Lion for the first time, I realised this was no longer simply my journey back to music.

It had become our journey.

Why Kickstarter?

From the very beginning, the ambition behind A Century in Sound was never simply to document these remarkable places. It was to experience, as closely as film allows, what it actually feels like to sit inside them. To feel that quiet anticipation as one record ends and another is about to begin. To hear music through the very same sound systems that generations of listeners have gathered around for decades. To feel, for a brief moment, like another customer in the room.

To achieve that, we made one decision that shaped the entire production. Everything you hear throughout the series has been recorded live through the sound systems of each establishment, capturing the unique acoustics, atmosphere and character of these remarkable spaces.

Jazz Kissa Eigakan, Tokyo. 

In most documentaries, music accompanies the story.

In A Century in Sound, the music is the story.

Every recording has been chosen with enormous care, not simply because it represents a particular artist or era, but because it belongs to a particular ongaku kissa, a particular moment in history, and a particular community of listeners. Hundreds of hours were spent listening inside these cafÃ©s, understanding not only which records best represented each establishment, but which ones held the deepest meaning for the people who have devoted their lives to them.

That commitment shaped every stage of the production, including one of its greatest challenges: music licensing. Across the six episodes, we need to secure 131 individual music and publishing licences, spanning more than a century of recorded music: from iconic performances of Beethoven and legends of jazz, to some of the defining Japanese recordings of the 1970s and 80s.

Now comes the final step.

To share A Century in Sound beyond film festivals, we must secure the licences that will allow the series to be experienced in listening bars, museums, on television and, ultimately, online. This Kickstarter will help us secure those licences and finally bring A Century in Sound to the audiences it was made for, introducing people around the world to Japanâ€™s extraordinary culture of dedicated listening.
